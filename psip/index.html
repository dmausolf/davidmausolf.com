Psychedelic therapy

What if the healing happens during the trip, not after?

A 2020 paper proposes a new way to do psychedelic therapy. It's called PSIP.

Here is the idea, one card at a time.

Based on “The PSIP Model” by Saj Razvi & Steven Elfrink
Journal of Psychedelic Psychiatry, June 2020

The usual way

Today, therapists mostly stay quiet.

The patient lies down. Eyeshades on. Soft music.

Two therapists sit nearby. They talk only if the patient wants to. Most don't.

The real talking happens the next day. That's called integration.

The big change

PSIP moves the work into the trip itself.

The therapist is active during the session.

The work runs through the body and the relationship, not just words.

If it goes well, the next day gets easy.

How the brain works

Your brain runs two kinds of mind.

One is old. We share it with dogs, lizards and fish.

One is new. It's what makes us human.

The paper's claim: much of who you are is stored in the old one.

The conductor

One network keeps the new mind in charge.

It's called the default mode network, or DMN.

Think of a conductor. It keeps the orchestra tidy by quieting the wilder, older mind.

The cost: we lose touch with the body's built-in repair systems.

What the drug does

Psychedelics loosen the conductor's grip.

Classic psychedelics hit one receptor, called 5-HT2A.

It's packed into the DMN. It's rare in the deep, old brain.

So the new mind loosens, and the old mind rises. Brain regions start talking to each other more.

A spectrum

Too tight or too loose both cause trouble.

A mind can be over-organized. Or under-organized.

Psychedelics push a tight mind toward loose, for a few hours.

That's why people with a history of psychosis are told not to do this work.

The body's alarm

Under threat, every mammal moves through the same four states.

Scientists back to Darwin have watched it. The paper calls it the defense cascade.

You don't choose these states. Your nervous system does.

Meet the zebra.

Calm. Then on edge.

The zebra grazes in the sun. Then the grass rustles.

A lion is out there. Not close. But the whole herd goes on alert.

State 2

Fight or flight.

The lion charges. Every zebra sprints for its life.

For people: war, car crashes. And for children: neglect or abuse at home.

A child can't fight back or run. So even “small” threats can push a child here.

States 3 and 4

When fighting fails, the body shuts down.

The lion catches the zebra. The zebra goes still.

The body releases its own opioids. Pain and feeling go numb.

The trap

From the outside, state 4 looks exactly like calm.

Both people say “I'm fine.” Therapists often mix them up.

One can feel their body. The other feels nothing.

The last person to know they're numb is the person who is.

Why it matters

Numbness can cancel out a psychedelic.

People with a lot of state 4 may feel sober, bored or sleepy, even on MDMA or psilocybin.

Without a therapist engaging them, the authors say, some might sleep through the peak.

The body's own opioids act like a brake.

The way back

The body knows how to come back down.

Wild mammals that survive a threat shift back on their own, without words or insight: 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 0.

It's automatic. Like sweating to cool down, or insulin after sugar.

In people, this return often gets stuck.

What gets in the way

Two things keep people stuck.

The way back is not pleasant. It means feeling what got trapped.

1. We avoid itour choice

We distract, breathe it away, think about something else. We hate losing control.
PSIP's technique targets this.

2. The DMNour wiring

The conductor keeps the body's repair system switched down.
The psychedelic targets this.

Selective inhibition

The core move: don't escape the feeling.

  1. A memory comes up. Your gut tightens. Breath gets short. Your neck pulls.
  2. You want to take a deep breath, or think about your vacation.
  3. The therapist says: notice that urge. Don't follow it. Stay with your gut.
  4. The feeling grows, peaks, and settles. On its own.

The therapist's two jobs

Set up the sleep. Then trust the dream.

Think about falling asleep. You can control the room: dark, quiet, still.

But once asleep, the dream runs itself.

PSIP works the same way. Firm about blocking the escape hatches. Then hands-off about whatever comes up.

Directivesteers

“Notice the urge to escape. Let it go. Come back to the feeling.”

Non-directivetrusts

Shaking, crying, images, anger. The body leads. The therapist follows.

Working with numbness

With numbness, the blank spot is the door.

Boredom. Flatness. Sleepiness. “Nothing is happening.”

That is the signal. Stay with it instead of looking for something exciting.

In weekly talk therapy this can take months. On a psychedelic, minutes to a full day. Then it cracks open.

Which medicine

Not every medicine opens numbness equally.

The authors were surprised by cannabis. Paired with this technique, it stopped being calming and started “excavating.”

Their Amsterdam program preps every client first with three cannabis sessions over two days:

Days 1–2 · three cannabis sessions
Day 3 · psilocybin
Psilocybinalso mescaline, ayahuasca

Can pass right over a numb person. Just water, flowers, shapes.

MDMA

Much better at reaching numbness, with the right focus.

The relationship

Most wounds come from people. So healing needs a person.

On a psychedelic, old relationships surface fast. You may see a parent in your therapist. That's called transference.

A PSIP therapist doesn't correct it. They play the role, so the body can process it.

They can also offer what was missing: warmth, attention, being seen.

The roadmap

A roadmap in three tiers.

“You have to be someone before you can be no one.”

Skipping to the mystical top, the authors argue, gives highs you can't hold onto.

PSIP mostly works on tiers 1 and 2.

Read with care

How to read this paper.

It's a strong idea. It is not proven yet.

  • It's a viewpoint piece, not a clinical trial. Claims come from the authors' clinical experience.
  • The authors founded PSIP and run its institute in Denver.
  • They call their own tier map “untested.”
  • Not for people with a history of psychosis.
  • Opening up numbness can be destabilizing. It needs support between sessions.
  • Laws on psychedelics and cannabis vary by place.

Recap

The whole idea in five lines.

Flip back anytime with “All cards.”

  1. Much of who you are lives in an old, wordless, body-based mind.
  2. Psychedelics loosen the newer mind and let the old one surface.
  3. Many symptoms are the body stuck in alarm (anxiety, panic) or shutdown (depression, numbness).
  4. The body can return to calm by itself, if you stop escaping the feeling.
  5. So the therapist works actively during the trip, through the body and the relationship.
